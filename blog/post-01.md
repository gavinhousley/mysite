### Split keyboards

24.09.26

I can type pretty fast on a keyboard, although by that I mean, I can type pretty fast on a keyboard _for me_. But after a long spell as a bookseller, hammering in ISBN numbers using the numerical keypad, as with many office workers I'm sure, I started to have wrist problems. So since retraining as a software developer, I figured I needed to finally jump in and purchase a split keyboard.

Like many men my age, the first step was to do a lot of research into the options and possibilities. The first result and concern seemed to be the PRICE. These keyboards were expensive. Used to the handy Magic Apple keyboards, it seemed a bit extortionate. But of course, if the majority of the population are happy with what they have, the obscure few are going to have to pay for it.

But what if I don't like it? This was the main problem. It's not like you can go to your local dev parts library and loan one.

So with a little searching I decided on a Lily58 from mechboards.co.uk. And then, like most new purchase adventures, I had to think about it for a while. And I'm glad I did. I did a bit more research and found some blog posts about the Lily58 — some liked it, loved it, and others not so much. Somewhere along the line I switched to the Corne keyboard instead. It had fewer keys, but apparently once you became familiar with layers it didn't really matter, and once the touch typing was there, you didn't even have to look at the keys. Really?

So shape decided upon, there were then key switches to choose. I have to say I nearly backed out at this point. Partly because I simply couldn't tell the difference between the keys, and also because I was thinking of wiring it up myself to save money. But after a bit of research I realised my soldering iron is very cheap and old — I'd need a new one, and with the added costs and the risk of melting my switches or putting together an expensive keyboard that didn't work in the end... I just thought I'd buy it complete.

So I went with a wired Corne keyboard with Lowprokb Sunset switches, as I wanted a low profile key — being used to Apple keyboards, I didn't want too much finger movement.

I went for it despite the cost and invested in my wrist health. As with all of these things, the freshness and newness of a whole new way of typing was very exciting at first — and of course the surging rainbow of LEDs helped with that. But then my typing was SO SLOW. It took a while to get used to. Checking the VIAL keyboard software constantly to see where keys were and in what layer. And then back to JavaScript — where were all the brackets? Arrow functions! But somehow, a few months later, I am now roughly back to the speed I was at before, but now my wrists are straight, instead of my right wrist having severe ulnar deviation despite my best attempts to correct it.

So what would I advise someone who hadn't tried it yet? I would say go for it — you'll soon learn how to use the layers, if you're someone who can persist past frustration. Compared to a laptop keyboard, or at least an Apple keyboard, you will be moving your fingers up and down a bit more, but conversely not as far afield as before. My other favourite thing is that because the keyboard is in two halves, it fits neatly into a small case with them side by side — unless you're likely to crush it, in which case I'd opt for a hard case.

The last thing: at some point all the LEDs switched off on my keyboard, and I think I had inadvertently switched them off somehow. Somewhere in the Mechboards help chat there is a post titled "black screen" — and the solution is fairly simple. You go into VIAL, the software that comes with the keyboard, and on one of your least used and emptier layers — in my case Layer 4 — you can add key functions for switching the LEDs on and off, as well as brightness adjustment if you prefer.
